Give Up What You Want for What You Want More

Benedict says success asks you to give up what you want for what you want more. The bill comes due in trade-offs, and most people never sign it.
July 21, 2026 · 2 min · Steven A. Rodríguez

Choose, and then stop mourning what you did not choose.

That is the cost Elsie Lincoln Benedict names in How to Get Anything You Want, and she does not let you dodge it. Success, she writes, requires a willingness to give up what you want for what you want more. Not what you do not want — what you genuinely want, set down on purpose, so your hands are free for the thing you want more.

She runs it through a dinner table. Some of the dishes you would like and could have are not good for you. Some are not worth cooking. And some simply do not combine with the things you want more. So you make a selection, and then you work toward it without circling back to grieve everything you decided to forego this time.

That last clause is where most people fail. They make the choice. Then they spend the energy meant for the chosen thing on quiet regret for the unchosen one. Half their effort goes to the goal, half goes to mourning the cake they set down. And a goal pursued with half your effort tends to come back as half a result.

This is not a lesson about deprivation. It is a lesson about clarity. Wanting many things is human and fine — until two of them pull in opposite directions, and then wanting both is just standing still with great sincerity. The willing person sorts the wants by weight, picks the heaviest, and lets the lighter ones go without a funeral.

Benedict frames the test plainly: are you giving up the goal for the cake, or the cake for the goal? Read that against your own week. Every time you trade the deep work for the easy hit, the build for the comfortable evening, the long thing for the quick thing, you are answering the question. You are telling yourself, in the only language that counts, which one you want more.

The trade itself is not the hard part. The hard part is signing it — choosing without keeping one hand on the door, committing to the thing you want more and letting the rest be genuinely gone.

So name the two wants pulling against each other, decide which one is heavier, and put the other down. Then get to work, and do not look back at the cake.
